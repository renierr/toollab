How a night is scored

Every night starts at 100 and loses points for each thing outside the range a standard adult sleep study reports. There is no weighting, no model and no learning — five independent checks, fixed deductions, floored at zero. This page is the whole algorithm, plus a scorer you can push around.

01 What the five checks read

Only two numbers come from the record itself; the rest are derived from the stage list.

QuantityHow it is derived
time in bed endTime − startTime. The session's whole span, including lying awake at either end.
asleep Sum of every stage minus the awake ones. When a writer supplies no stages at all it falls back to the record's own asleepMinutes, and failing that the entire span counts as sleep.
efficiency asleep ÷ time in bed, clamped to 0–1.
deep share · REM share Each stage type's total as a share of time asleep, not of time in bed. Null when no stages carry deep or REM, and a null share is skipped rather than penalised.
awake Total of the awake, awake_in_bed and out_of_bed stages.

The verdict is a straight cut on the total: ≥ 80 good, 60–79 fair, below that poor. Worst case is 100 − 25 − 20 − 10 − 10 − 15 = 20, so the floor at zero never actually fires.

The number of awakenings is counted but never scored. It is shown next to the awake total, and it counts every awake stage — including one before sleep onset. Only the awake minutes cost points.

Awake minutes are not a slider. They are time in bed − asleep here, which is what a stage list that tiles the whole session produces. A writer that leaves gaps between stages would report less awake time than that difference implies.

Three wakes cost nothing. They add up to 15 minutes, which is inside the 30-minute allowance, and the count itself is not scored at all. The only two points lost are for sleeping an hour under seven, and for more deep sleep than the reference range — which is the one deduction most people would not expect.

05 Where the scoring is weak

  • Duration is under-weighted. An hour short of the guidance costs 10 points, so a 6-hour night still lands in Good as long as nothing else fires. Duration is the single measure with the strongest evidence behind it, and here it can be outweighed by two stage-share quibbles.
  • The bands are cliffs, not curves. 5 h 59 m costs 25 and 6 h 01 m costs 10, a 15-point swing across two minutes. Nothing interpolates.
  • More deep sleep is penalised. −5 for exceeding 23 % reads as a defect on a card that otherwise means "worse". It is really "unusual", not "bad".
  • The REM window is 5 points wide (20–25 %), narrower than the night-to-night variation of most sleepers, so the check fires on noise.
  • Efficiency shares an input with duration. Both derive from asleep, so a short night measured against a short time in bed is scored twice on the same fact.
  • Awakenings are counted and discarded. Three brief wakes and one hour-long wake are the same to the score if the totals match, which is not how sleep continuity works.
  • No sleep-onset latency. Time between going to bed and the first non-awake stage is never isolated; it only shows up inside efficiency.

None of this makes the number wrong — it is exactly what the rules say — but it is a coarse heuristic over population ranges, not a measurement. It should be read as "nothing stood out" versus "several things did", not as a grade.
